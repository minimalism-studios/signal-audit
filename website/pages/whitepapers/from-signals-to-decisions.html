From Signals to Decisions

Designing trustworthy AI-assisted operational intelligence through evidence, context, and human judgment.

Executive Summary

Modern operational environments generate extensive telemetry: metrics, logs, traces, alerts, and events. These records reveal aspects of system behavior, but they do not automatically establish what an operator should conclude or do. The introduction of AI-assisted interpretation creates an opportunity to connect technical evidence with human decisions. It also creates a new reliability problem: an explanation can sound convincing without being adequately supported.

An operational interpretation is not a decision. It is an evidence-grounded explanation intended to help a responsible person make a better decision.

This paper proposes a framework for evaluating the trustworthiness of AI-assisted operational interpretations. It considers the provenance of evidence, contextual relevance, uncertainty, decision authority, and measurable interpretation quality. These requirements complement established reliability practices and the emphasis on trustworthy AI risk management in the NIST AI Risk Management Framework [1].

The central argument is that usefulness alone is insufficient. An interpretation must also be traceable, appropriately qualified, and safe to challenge. A recommendation that saves time but misrepresents evidence may make an operational response worse. Conversely, a concise explanation that identifies what is known, what remains uncertain, and what to investigate next can improve judgment without claiming authority it does not possess.

The framework presented here is a research proposal. It is not a claim that every described evaluation, correlation, or decision-support capability is currently implemented in Signal Audit.

The Interpretation-to-Decision Gap

Observation Does Not Determine Action

Consider a production service whose request latency exceeds an alert threshold. The alert establishes that a monitored condition occurred. It does not, by itself, establish the underlying cause, customer impact, or appropriate response. The same symptom may accompany resource contention, dependency degradation, increased traffic, or a deployment regression.

An AI-generated explanation can reduce the effort required to organize available evidence. But the explanation is a new analytical artifact, not an additional observation of the system. Its claims must therefore be evaluated separately from the telemetry that prompted it.

Three Distinct Responsibilities

Operational decision-making benefits from separating three activities. Monitoring establishes observable conditions. Interpretation describes their potential significance. Decision-making determines what action, if any, should follow. Combining these activities without explicit boundaries can turn an uncertain interpretation into an unjustified action.

Decision Relevance

A technically accurate summary may still be operationally unhelpful. Repeating that latency exceeded a threshold adds little if the alert already states that fact. A useful interpretation identifies the relevant context, the likely scope of impact, evidence that supports or contradicts candidate explanations, and the next question worth answering.

Google's SRE guidance emphasizes actionable monitoring and alerting tied to service reliability objectives [4]. AI-assisted interpretation should be assessed against the same practical concern: whether information helps operators recognize and respond to meaningful conditions.

02 / RESEARCH

Evidence Before Explanation

Provenance as a Design Requirement

Every material claim in an operational interpretation should be attributable to identifiable evidence. Relevant attributes include source system, event identifier, timestamp, environment, service, observed measurement, threshold, and available correlation identifiers. The exact fields depend on the monitoring integration and the event type.

Provenance makes a finding reviewable. An operator should be able to distinguish a value received from a monitoring system from a conclusion generated by an interpretation model. Where source evidence is unavailable, the interpretation should disclose that limitation rather than fabricate a supporting record.

Evidence Completeness

An alert payload may contain a metric value and threshold without including deployment history, request traces, dependency health, or customer-impact measurements. Such an event may support a statement that a threshold was exceeded, but not a definitive explanation of why.

The appropriate response to incomplete evidence is narrower interpretation, not greater rhetorical confidence. A finding may identify a plausible investigative direction while explicitly withholding a root-cause conclusion.

Source Facts and Generated Claims

A useful evidence record separates observed facts, derived calculations, hypotheses, and recommendations. For example, a recorded latency value is a source fact; a change in latency across two measured windows is a derived observation; a suspected database bottleneck is a hypothesis unless additional evidence supports it.

This separation also helps prevent the repeated presentation of generated explanations as if they were independent measurements. A prior model summary is not a substitute for the original telemetry.

Context, Correlation, and Causality

Context Changes Meaning

An elevated error rate may have different implications depending on the affected service, environment, duration, traffic volume, and user impact. A short-lived spike in staging is not equivalent to sustained errors in a customer-facing production dependency.

Context should therefore be relevant to the operational question rather than collected indiscriminately. Excessive unrelated data can obscure the evidence needed for a timely decision and increase privacy or access risks.

Correlation Across Systems

Distributed telemetry can connect related activity through identifiers and propagated execution context. OpenTelemetry describes mechanisms for carrying context across service boundaries and correlating signals [3]. Such mechanisms provide a foundation for reconstructing related behavior when instrumentation is available.

A common trace identifier can establish that records belong to a related execution path. It does not automatically establish that one observed condition caused another. Likewise, a latency increase following a deployment is a temporal association until corroborating evidence supports a causal explanation.

Testing Competing Explanations

Trustworthy interpretation should consider more than one plausible explanation when the evidence permits ambiguity. A useful finding might report that dependency latency, saturation, and deployment timing remain competing hypotheses, then identify which additional measurement would help distinguish them.

This approach favors falsifiable investigative questions over prematurely definitive root-cause statements.

Uncertainty and Confidence

Confidence Is Not Fluency

Language models can express uncertain conclusions in polished, authoritative prose. The apparent confidence of the wording is not evidence of analytical accuracy. Operational systems must therefore distinguish stylistic certainty from measured reliability.

The NIST Generative AI Profile provides a broader risk-management context for assessing generative AI capabilities and limitations [2]. Applying that perspective to operational interpretation means treating unsupported claims and missing context as evaluation concerns.

Three Sources of Uncertainty

Evidence uncertainty arises when relevant records are missing, delayed, sampled, or inconsistent. Analytical uncertainty arises when multiple explanations fit the available evidence. Decision uncertainty arises when operational priorities, risk tolerance, or consequences remain unresolved.

These conditions should not be collapsed into a single unexplained confidence percentage. If numerical confidence is used, its meaning and calibration should be established through evaluation against representative cases.

When to Abstain

An interpretation system should be permitted to state that the evidence is insufficient. Abstention may be more reliable than generating a detailed but speculative explanation. Appropriate outputs include a factual summary, explicit limitations, and a request for the next relevant evidence source.

Abstention from causal interpretation does not require silence about the underlying alert. Operators can still receive the observed condition and any established urgency while the explanation remains unresolved.

05 / RESEARCH

Human Judgment and Decision Authority

Interpretation as Decision Support

AI-assisted interpretation can organize evidence and propose investigative steps, but responsibility for consequential operational actions must remain clearly assigned. Restarting services, changing access policies, suppressing alerts, or modifying production configuration may require authorization beyond the scope of an interpretation system.

Reviewability and Contestability

Operators should be able to inspect the basis of a finding, challenge its assumptions, and reject its recommendation. A useful interface presents supporting evidence and uncertainty in a way that does not force the reviewer to accept the model's framing.

Human oversight is meaningful only when reviewers have sufficient context and practical authority to disagree. A nominal approval step does not compensate for missing evidence or an opaque recommendation.

Escalation and Action Boundaries

The system should distinguish low-risk investigative suggestions from actions that alter production state. Recommendations should identify the intended owner, prerequisites, and potential consequences where appropriate. Any future automation would require separately defined permissions, controls, and testing.

These are proposed safeguards for trustworthy decision support, not claims that Signal Audit autonomously executes remediation.

Evaluating Interpretation Quality

Evaluation Must Be Evidence-Based

A useful evaluation program compares generated findings with source records and expert-reviewed expectations. It should include ordinary alerts, incomplete payloads, contradictory signals, misleading correlations, and cases in which the correct behavior is to abstain.

A Proposed Evaluation Rubric

Six dimensions provide a practical starting point: factual accuracy, evidence traceability, contextual relevance, uncertainty handling, decision usefulness, and respect for action boundaries.

Factual accuracy asks whether source values and conditions are represented correctly. Traceability asks whether material claims can be verified. Contextual relevance asks whether the explanation addresses the operational situation. Uncertainty handling assesses unsupported certainty and appropriate abstention.

Decision usefulness examines whether the output helps an operator identify a reasonable next step. Action-boundary compliance tests whether the interpretation preserves human and system authority. A serious failure in accuracy or authority should not be offset merely by a high score for clarity.

Representative Test Cases

An evaluation set should include repeated alerts, deployment-associated changes, partial evidence, irrelevant metadata, inconsistent timestamps, and superficially persuasive but unsupported root-cause hypotheses. Cases should reflect the event types and environments actually supported by the product.

Measuring Improvement

Potential measures include unsupported-claim rate, evidence-link completeness, expert agreement, appropriate-abstention rate, reviewer correction frequency, and time required to identify a useful investigative next step.

These metrics require defined test sets, reviewer guidance, and baselines. This paper reports no experimental results or validated performance improvements for Signal Audit.

An Operational Decision Framework

The proposed workflow connects evidence to judgment through six distinguishable stages. It is a reference framework for system design and evaluation rather than a description of a fully deployed agent.

1. Receive and Preserve

Accept a supported operational event, validate its structure, and preserve relevant source attributes. Missing required fields should be recorded rather than silently invented.

2. Establish the Observed Condition

Identify what the evidence directly establishes: affected service, environment, measurement, threshold, timing, and event status. Keep this factual account separate from any explanation of cause.

3. Add Relevant Context

Incorporate authorized supporting evidence when available. Record which context was used and distinguish unavailable evidence from evidence that contradicts a hypothesis.

4. Interpret with Explicit Limits

Generate a concise interpretation that identifies observed facts, plausible explanations, uncertainty, and unanswered questions. Abstain from unsupported root-cause claims.

5. Present Decision Options

Offer proportionate investigative next steps with supporting rationale. Do not represent proposed actions as approved, executed, or guaranteed to resolve the condition.

6. Review and Learn

Allow authorized operators to review, correct, and evaluate the finding. Capture feedback in a controlled process that can inform subsequent testing and improvements without altering the historical source evidence.

Trustworthy operational intelligence connects evidence to explanation, explanation to judgment, and judgment to accountable action.

Illustrative Example

Suppose an alert reports sustained production API latency above a configured threshold. The system preserves the reported metric and time window. It notes that no dependency trace or deployment history is present in the supplied evidence. Instead of asserting a database failure, it recommends examining dependency latency and recent changes. The operator decides which investigation to pursue.

The example demonstrates a design principle: a bounded, verifiable interpretation can be more operationally valuable than an elaborate but unsupported diagnosis.

08 / RESEARCH

Conclusion

The transition from operational signals to decisions is not accomplished simply by generating explanations. It requires a disciplined relationship between recorded evidence, contextual analysis, uncertainty, and accountable human judgment.

AI-assisted interpretation can help reduce the cognitive effort of organizing operational evidence. Its value, however, must be demonstrated through traceability, factual accuracy, appropriate restraint, and measurable usefulness.

A reliable system should make it easier to verify what happened, identify what remains unknown, and determine the next appropriate investigation. It should not convert missing evidence into confident conclusions or replace established operational authority.

The goal is not to make AI sound certain. The goal is to make operational decisions better informed.
